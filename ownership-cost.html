5-year ownership cost

Updated October 3, 2026.

Built by Jeremy Panasuk.

Use this when you already know a running load and you want five-year cash, not a watt class. The classes themselves are on portable vs standby. Every dollar field on this form is an input you type. The defaults are not a price index.

What the button adds

GS.tco in assets/lab.js always uses 5 years. It does not read a years field.

  • Gasoline gal/h = running watts ÷ 7,000. Same site rule as the storm runbook, applied to running watts, not to the home calculator’s recommended watts.
  • Annual portable fuel dollars = gal/h × outage hours per year × the $/gal you type.
  • Oil charges = at least one, otherwise the engine hours over 5 years divided by 50 and rounded up, then times $12. Both the 50 and the $12 are the script. Honda’s EU2200i manual calls for an oil change at the first 20 hours and then on its maintenance schedule, whose columns include 50 hours and 100 hours. Generac’s GP6500/GP8000E manual says the first oil change is at 30 hours, then every 100 hours or every year. The $12 is not a quoted oil price.
  • Portable five-year total = purchase + annual fuel × 5 + the oil charges.
  • Standby five-year total = installed price + yearly service × 5. There is no fuel term on the standby side. Natural gas or propane for a standby is not priced here.
  • Break-even hours = (standby install − portable purchase) ÷ ($/gal × gal/h). That is cumulative running hours, not hours per year. It does not change when you change the outage-hours field.

EIA lists 120,214 Btu per gallon of motor gasoline. That heat content is not a price, and it is not the ÷ 7,000 rule. Kilowatt-hours, which are a different unit, are on the kW to kWh calculator.

Worked clicks

The form opens at 2,210 running watts, 18 outage hours per year, $3.40 per gallon, a $1,400 portable, a $12,500 standby install, and $280 a year of service. 2,210 W is the sequencer’s stored well-ranch always-on running sum, described on the sequencer page. The dollar figures are whatever is in the boxes.

  • Gal/h = 2,210 / 7,000 = 0.3157, shown as 0.32.
  • Annual fuel = 0.3157 × 18 × 3.40 = $19.32, shown as $19.
  • Engine hours in five years = 18 × 5 = 90. Oil charges = ceil(90 / 50) × $12 = 2 × $12 = $24.
  • Portable = 1,400 + 19.32 × 5 + 24 = $1,520.61, shown as $1,521. Standby = 12,500 + 280 × 5 = $13,900. The button says portable is cheaper.
  • Break-even hours = (12,500 − 1,400) / (3.40 × 0.3157) = 10,341 hours on the rounded display. 10,341 / 18 is 574.5 years at that annual rate.

Second click: change only the outage hours to 120 and leave the other boxes. Annual fuel becomes 2,210 / 7,000 × 120 × 3.40 = $128.81, shown as $129. Five-year engine hours = 600. Oil charges = ceil(600 / 50) × 12 = 12 × 12 = $144. Portable = 1,400 + 128.81 × 5 + 144 = $2,188.06, shown as $2,188. Standby stays $13,900. Break-even stays 10,341 hours, because that line does not use the 120. One hundred twenty hours a year for five years is 600 hours, still far under 10,341. The runbook is where those 120 hours become cans.

Limits and a common mistake

Reading 10,341 as “hours per year” makes a standby look cheaper than this arithmetic says. The 10,341 is total portable runtime. The outage-hours box only scales fuel and oil.

The comparison leaves out labor, noise, and standby fuel. A heavier open-frame and a neighbor’s window are the lot-line question, not a dollar in this total. A load that needs a larger machine changes the purchase prices you type; the script does not look up either price. Permanent installation of a standby takes a licensed electrician and a permit where local code requires one.
